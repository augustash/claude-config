# ADA Title I: employers and labor organizations (42 U.S.C. 12111–12112)

Read from the statute (Cornell LII, 42 U.S.C. 12111, 12112, 2000e) and the Department of
Justice's web guidance, 2026-10-24. Not legal advice; the client's counsel decides what they
rely on.

**Who it reaches.** A *covered entity* is "an employer, employment agency, labor organization,
or joint labor-management committee" (§ 12111(2)). An employer counts at 15 or more employees
(§ 12111(5)). A labor organization takes Title VII's meaning (§ 12111(7) → § 2000e(e)): it
counts if it "maintains or operates a hiring hall or hiring office which procures employees for
an employer or procures for employees opportunities to work", or has 15 or more members.

**What it covers.** No covered entity may discriminate against a qualified individual "in regard
to job application procedures, the hiring, advancement, or discharge of employees, employee
compensation, job training, and other terms, conditions, and privileges of employment"
(§ 12112(a)). Arrangements count too: a relationship "with an employment or referral agency,
labor union, an organization providing fringe benefits … or an organization providing training
and apprenticeship programs" (§ 12112(b)(2)). So the pages that weigh are the ones people use
to **apply, join, get referred to work, train, and reach their benefits**, not the whole site
equally.

**There is no web regulation and no deadline.** Title I has no web technical standard. The
EEOC enforces it, not the DOJ. The DOJ's 2022 web guidance (written for Titles II and III)
says it "does not have a regulation setting out detailed standards" and names WCAG and the
Section 508 Standards as the existing technical standards that "provide helpful guidance". Use
**WCAG 2.1 AA as the yardstick** and say so plainly. Never write that a regulation requires it,
and never attribute the yardstick to Title I itself.

**Accommodation is a separate duty** (§ 12112(b)(5)(A)): an applicant or member who can't use a
page has to be accommodated another way. A site that meets the yardstick makes that rare; it
doesn't replace it.

**State law can add to it.** Most states have a human rights act that reaches the same
activities, often for smaller employers or with wider remedies. Look up the client's state and
put what it adds in the project's memory, not here.

**A client can sit in two rule sets.** Title III's public accommodations include "a … private
school, or other place of education" (§ 12181(7)(J)), so an employer or union that runs a
training program open to the public may owe Title III for those pages as well. Check what the
site offers to people who aren't applicants or members.

**How it shapes the plan:** rank first what stops someone applying, joining, getting referred or
enrolling in training, then reach across the site. With no deadline, the year plan sets its own
pace, front-loaded.
